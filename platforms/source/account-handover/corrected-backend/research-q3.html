SOE-Q003 · version 1.0 · 27 September 2026

What really needs a shared record, and who remains responsible?

Research status: Open question · Novelty unestablished · No field trial completed

The question

In a concrete arrangement between a resource operator, a participant, and a customer, what must be recorded or verified—and can ordinary controllers, databases, signed records, and contracts do the job?

Why it matters

Permission, physical delivery, payment, and responsibility are different things. Adding a ledger can make some records easier to inspect without making a measurement true or a machine obey.

What is currently known

There are established mechanisms for delegated authority, auditable statements, and financial settlement. Cryptographic verification can check a statement against a specified model; it cannot establish that the model completely describes the physical world. No need for a new token or blockchain has been demonstrated in this research.

What remains unknown

Whether a particular use case needs a trust property that a simpler arrangement cannot provide at acceptable cost—and what responsibility remains with operators when evidence conflicts or delivery fails.

Contributions that would help

  • A worked example of the parties, their disagreement, and the evidence needed to resolve it.
  • A conventional solution that meets the same requirements.
  • A precise explanation of what independent verification would add.
  • An objection from someone affected by the allocation, including people who are not market participants.

A useful first contribution: “Who would I have to trust here, and what could I do if they were wrong?”

What would change our minds

If ordinary infrastructure supplies the required property, use it. A reproducible counterexample to those alternatives could justify evaluating additional cryptographic machinery. That still would not establish a need for a new chain or token.

Start with these sources

  • Macaroons — delegated authority with restrictions. Reading status: author source read. Not independently reproduced. A copyable credential is not by itself a conserved spending record.
  • IETF SCITT architecture — a draft transparency architecture. Reading status: current draft inspected. Not a finalized standard. Not independently reproduced. Transparency receipts describe a recorded statement; they do not make an issuer honest or a measurement true.
  • PJM’s FTR fact sheet — an example of a financial instrument distinct from physical delivery. Reading status: official description of the financial role inspected. Not independently reproduced. An FTR is not a physical right to inject or withdraw electricity.

Prompt above the response box

What needs to be trusted or checked, and what is the simplest arrangement that works?

Scope
Included: what must be recorded or verified among an operator, a participant, and a customer, and whether ordinary controllers, databases, signed records, and contracts suffice. Excluded: treating a ledger, price, meter, or token as a substitute for physical control, measurement, or legal responsibility.
Owner
Editorial owner when a record changes: Chris Taylor, or a reviewer he designates. That names a person, not a panel. Intake is not open, so no contribution is in review.
Disposition
Open as a question about a concrete arrangement. A new token, DAO, blockchain, or settlement layer as a necessary enabler is unsupported. Novelty unestablished. No field trial completed.
Claims
Fact about the research to date: no need for a new token or blockchain has been demonstrated. Fact about cryptography within a specified model: verification can check a statement against that model and cannot establish that the model completely describes the physical world. Unknown whether a particular use case needs a trust property simpler infrastructure cannot provide at acceptable cost. Unsupported Meridian as a new sovereign architecture, on the evidence checked.
Assumptions
Permission, physical delivery, payment, and responsibility stay separate layers. Mutual distrust is not by itself a proof that a blockchain is necessary or adequate. First architecture to test, when there is one accountable operator and durable reservations: an ordinary transactional database and local controllers.
Baseline
Ordinary controllers, databases, signed records, and contracts. Macaroons for delegated authority with restrictions. A draft transparency architecture such as SCITT when the need is a checkable statement, not physical truth. Financial instruments such as FTRs stay distinct from physical delivery.
Contributions
None. Contributions are not open yet.
Objections
Unresolved: ordinary infrastructure may already supply the required property, in which case it should be used. A counterexample to those alternatives would still not establish a new chain or token. No contributor objection is on file.
Tests
Proposed, not started. No cryptographic machinery is being built from this page.
Results
No field trial. No demonstrated unmet trust property.
